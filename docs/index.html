Spades Prototype Lab

Two small prototypes, each built to answer one question about Spades on mobile. Both play on a phone right now, both log what players do, and both run on one rules engine that can sit on a server without a rewrite.

Prototype 01

Spades Blitz

Open on your phone

If a Spades match fits in three minutes, do more players hit Rematch?

Why ask it
A standard game to 500 can run 20 minutes or more, which is hard to start in a queue or on a short break. A rematch tap is the cleanest sign that a player wants more of the same thing.
What's in
Four hands of five tricks, team bidding with Nil and bags, 8-second turns that auto-play your lowest legal card, sudden death on a tie, three bots, and a suggested bid so new players aren't stuck. A standard-length control arm (13 tricks × 3 hands) runs on the same code.
What I cut
Matchmaking, accounts, coins, chat and anything cosmetic beyond readable tricks. None of it changes whether a short match feels good.
What it measures
Match length, rematch rate per arm, auto-plays per match (is 8 seconds too tight?), mid-match exits and tab closes, plus a one-tap "how did the length feel" vote at the end.
Decision rule
Progress if Blitz beats the control on rematch rate by 10 points or more across 30 finished matches per arm, and fewer than a third of players vote "too short". Iterate (6 hands, or 10-second turns) if rematches rise but the vote says too short, or auto-plays pass one per match. Stop if there's no lift.
Status
Bot-tested, ready for players

Prototype 02

Spades Run

Open on your phone

Does a run structure with rule-bending upgrades give solo Spades players a reason to start another session?

Why ask it
Solo play against bots is usually a waiting room for PvP. A run gives it a goal of its own and a reason to come back, the way Balatro did for poker hands.
What's in
Five tables with rising targets, two seven-card hands per table, nine Edges (pick one of three after each clear), two retry chips, a boss table with its own rules, and a best-run record.
What I cut
Meta-progression, unlock trees, currency and art beyond the felt. The question is whether the core loop pulls people into a second run, and none of that answers it.
What it measures
Run-again rate, tables cleared, which table ends runs, table length, pick rate for every Edge when offered, retry use, and a one-tap difficulty vote.
Decision rule
Progress if 40% or more of finished runs lead straight into another run. Iterate on the Edge list if any Edge is picked more than 70% of the time it's offered (too strong) or less than 10% (dead weight), or if most runs die at the same table. Stop if run-again stays under 20%.
Status
Tuned and bot-tested, ready for players

How it's built

  • One rules engine with no UI code. Both games, all bots and the simulation harness run the same file.
  • Hidden information stays hidden. Clients only ever receive a redacted view for their own seat, and the engine throws on any illegal action. Put it inside a Colyseus or Nakama room and it's server-authoritative as it stands.
  • Bots can't cheat. They decide from the same per-seat view a human gets. Difficulty is one skill number per seat.
  • Every match replays from its seed, and the seed is logged with the match, so any odd playtest moment can be reproduced.
  • The harness caught a real bug. In 13-card hands the bots were underbidding by 2.4 tricks per team, which made the control arm feel wrong. After a fix they take about 0.6 more than they bid, which is close to real tables.
  • Analytics are one function. Events are stored on the device for now and can be copied from each lobby; pointing track() at PostHog or GameAnalytics is a one-line change.
  • Built AI-first. An AI coding agent wrote most of the code. The questions, cuts, decision rules and tuning targets on this page are what steered it, and that loop is what I'd bring to the role.

Tuning Spades Run

Targets came from simulation rather than guesswork: 3,000 two-hand tables per rung, with rival skill and the number of Edges rising as they would in a real run.

With two retry chips that works out to roughly one full clear in three runs for a solid player. Real playtests will move these numbers, and the harness makes re-tuning a two-minute job.

If one graduates

The production team gets the engine with the rules written as code, the event schema, the simulation harness for balancing, and the playtest results with the decision that came out of them. The UI is throwaway by design and doesn't need to survive.
